How LLMs Actually Work — And Why You Can Build With Them Today

August 2026 · Rajender Karra

I am still learning AI engineering, but one idea became clear very early: everything that feels magical about AI is really a very powerful pattern executed at scale. At its core, a large language model is simply predicting the next chunk of text based on the context it has seen.

That idea sounds simple, but it is the foundation of chatbots, code assistants, summaries, search tools, and modern AI products. Once you understand that, the rest starts making sense.

Why now?

AI is not a new idea. Research in this space has been around for decades. What changed is the combination of scale, compute, data, and productization. Transformers changed the game, and the public launch of chat-driven systems made the technology available to everyone.

Today, I do not need a massive lab or a PhD to experiment with a real AI workflow. I can call an API, provide context, and get an answer. That is one of the most exciting technical shifts of our time.

What is an LLM really?

A model does not read words in the way a human does. It reads tokens — pieces of text that are grouped into manageable chunks. These tokens are scored based on probability, and the model picks the next likely token to continue the sequence.

This repeated process is how text is generated. The same idea is used for code generation, summarization, translation, and structured answers. The core mechanism is still prediction, just on a much larger and more sophisticated scale.

Temperature and creativity

The model chooses tokens based on probability, and temperature controls how adventurous or safe that choice becomes. Lower temperature keeps the output more deterministic. Higher temperature adds more variation and surprise.

This matters because AI engineering is not just about calling the model; it is about choosing the right behavior for the problem. A support assistant should be reliable and controlled. A creative brainstorming tool should be more exploratory.

No memory between calls

One important truth is that models do not remember previous conversations by default. Every call is a new request that includes the context you want the model to consider. This is why chat applications often send prior messages back in the request.

In practice, this means AI engineering involves careful context management. You provide the right inputs, the right instructions, and the right history to keep the interaction useful and coherent.

How the model learns

Another fascinating idea is that models learn through self-supervision. Instead of needing a giant pool of manually labelled examples, they can train on a huge amount of existing text by hiding a word or phrase and predicting it.

Over time, this process gives the model an internal understanding of syntax, semantics, and the patterns that make language coherent. The result is not a traditional rule engine, but a very capable statistical model that can generalize across many tasks.

What you can build

Once I understood this, the most important question changed: not “is AI magic?” but “what useful thing can I build with it today?” The answer is broader than people imagine.

  • chat experiences
  • summarizers and content tools
  • code assistants
  • search and retrieval workflows
  • document understanding and analysis
  • task-based agents and multi-step automation

The pattern is consistent: connect to a model, provide the right context, get a useful response, and wrap it in a simple interface or workflow.

The key takeaway

LLMs are not mysterious black boxes; they are practical, powerful systems built on prediction, context, and probability. The accessibility of these tools today is one of the biggest technical opportunities for builders.

This is why I am learning AI engineering in public: not to chase hype, but to understand how these systems work and how to build useful things with them.
